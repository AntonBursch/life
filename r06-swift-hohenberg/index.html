R6 — Swift–Hohenberg

One scalar field. One equation. One knob. Below the knob's zero, the field decays to nothing. Above zero, the field spontaneously chooses a wavelength and tiles the plane with stripes or labyrinths. R4 made patterns from two reacting chemicals; R5 made patterns from a fluid being heated. This rung shows what they were both an instance of, and it does it with the simplest possible field that still has the move.

10 0.100

r is the bifurcation knob. The interesting threshold sits exactly at r = 0. The natural wavelength of the pattern, when one exists, is 2π in these units — about six cells.

the claim

R4 needed two chemicals, four rate constants, and a specific reaction. R5 needed a fluid, a temperature gradient, gravity, and a streamfunction. They look like very different setups, but right at their thresholds they do the same thing in the same way. Both of them are, near onset, just this equation in disguise: a scalar field with a linear growth rate, a wavelength-selecting term that prefers a particular spatial frequency, and a cubic saturator. That's the whole machine. Whenever a substrate is poised between "uniform" and "patterned" states, you can change variables and arrive here. R6 is not another way to make patterns. It is the doorway R4 and R5 both walked through.

try this

  1. Start subcritical. Click subcritical (r=-0.2). The canvas seeds noise and then sweeps itself flat. Variance falls to zero. The system has no reason to organise.
  2. Cross zero. Click near onset (r=0.02). Now the noise refuses to die. Faint bands appear and slowly sharpen. The threshold is exactly r = 0. Below it the field is dead; above it the field commits.
  3. Let it choose a wavelength. Click stripes (r=0.1). The field locks into stripes spaced about six cells apart. You did not tell it that wavelength — the equation did.
  4. Labyrinths. Click labyrinth (r=0.3). The stripes can't sit straight any more; they bend, meet, and tile the plane in a fingerprint pattern. Same wavelength, more freedom in how to use it.
  5. Push it. Click strong (r=0.6). The pattern saturates near ±1 and the cubic does its job: u never blows up. The pattern is loud but stable.
  6. Slide r slowly across zero. Watch the variance. It does not creep — it lifts off a hard floor exactly at zero, like a square root. That lift-off is the bifurcation. Every "pattern-forming" system in physics has the same shape near its threshold.

what to look for

  • The threshold is sharp. Below r = 0 there is no pattern; above r = 0 there is. The transition is qualitative, not gradual.
  • The wavelength is intrinsic. The stripes are about six cells across whether you seed from random noise, from a single bright dot, or from anything else. The equation prefers one spatial frequency.
  • The cubic keeps the world finite. Linear growth alone would blow up. The −u³ term puts a ceiling on how loud the pattern can get. Every pattern-forming substrate in nature does this in some form, because anything that didn't would have already destroyed itself.

why this rung matters

R4 and R5 each looked like a self-contained story: a chemistry story and a fluids story. R6 says they are the same story. Near the onset of pattern formation, you can rewrite both of them — and many others, including the convective patterns of clouds, the buckling of thin shells, vegetation stripes on hillsides, retinal development — as this one PDE with a tuned r. The mechanism is universal; the substrate is what changes.

That's the key idea for everything above this rung. The living world is not a stack of unrelated tricks. It's a stack of the same handful of moves, performed in different substrates. R6 is the cleanest place to see one of those moves stripped to its bones.
